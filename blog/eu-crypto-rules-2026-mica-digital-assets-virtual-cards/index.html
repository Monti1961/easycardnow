EU Crypto Rules in 2026: What MiCA Means for Digital Assets and Virtual Cards

The EU crypto market entered a new compliance phase in 2026. MiCA does not regulate every virtual card itself, but it changes the regulatory environment for crypto-asset issuers and service providers, while payment and card services remain subject to their own rules.

What changed in 2026?

The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), Regulation (EU) 2023/1114, created harmonised rules for specified crypto-assets and crypto-asset services. ESMA describes MiCA as a framework covering transparency, disclosure, authorisation, supervision and market integrity. In 2026, the end of the transitional period for certain providers became an important compliance milestone: in many EU markets, providers relying on national transitional arrangements had to move to the MiCA framework by 1 July 2026.

That does not mean that every crypto product suddenly became a "MiCA product". The legal classification depends on the activity, the asset and the provider.

Does MiCA regulate virtual cards?

Not directly. A virtual card is a payment instrument or card product, while MiCA is focused on crypto-assets and crypto-asset services. A service that lets a user fund a card with USDT or another digital asset can therefore involve several regulatory layers.

  • Crypto layer: buying, exchanging, transferring or custody of crypto-assets may involve MiCA-regulated services.
  • Payment layer: issuing or providing payment instruments can fall under EU payment-services and electronic-money rules, depending on the structure.
  • Compliance layer: KYC, AML and transaction-monitoring requirements can apply to the relevant provider and service.

Why users may see more KYC and transaction checks

Regulated financial and crypto services increasingly need to identify customers, understand the purpose and source of funds where required, screen for sanctions and monitor transactions for suspicious activity. This can mean that a virtual-card onboarding process asks for identity information before the card is issued or funded.

EU rules on transfers of funds and crypto-assets also introduced information requirements commonly associated with the Travel Rule. The exact obligations depend on the parties and transaction structure, but users should expect more structured compliance checks rather than fewer.

What about USDT and stablecoins?

MiCA contains specific rules for asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens. Not every token marketed as a stablecoin has the same legal status, and the fact that an asset is called "USDT" or "stablecoin" does not by itself tell a user which regulatory category applies to a particular service.

For card users, the practical point is simple: check which assets and networks the service actually supports, and follow the current deposit instructions. Do not assume that an asset accepted by one service is automatically accepted by another.

What this means when using a crypto-funded virtual card

  1. Check the provider. Look for clear legal information, terms, fees and support contacts.
  2. Understand KYC. A legitimate regulated or regulated-partner service may require identity verification.
  3. Check the asset and network. Sending USDT on an unsupported blockchain can result in loss of funds.
  4. Read the card terms. Merchant categories, countries, limits and transaction types can be restricted.
  5. Keep records. Save transaction confirmations, card statements and relevant crypto transaction IDs.
  6. Do not confuse a card with a bank account. The legal nature of the service depends on the provider and product structure.

Is a virtual card automatically legal because it uses crypto?

No. The use of a virtual card does not by itself make a service compliant or non-compliant. Compliance depends on the provider, its authorisations or regulated partners, the services actually supplied, the jurisdictions involved and the applicable rules.

Likewise, the presence of a KYC process is not by itself proof that a service is authorised. Users should verify the provider's legal information and, where relevant, its regulatory status.

What users should check in 2026

  • Who is the actual service provider?
  • What company and jurisdiction are named in the terms?
  • Which crypto-assets and networks are supported?
  • When is KYC required?
  • What are the card, conversion and transaction fees?
  • What countries and merchant categories are restricted?
  • How are complaints, chargebacks and disputed transactions handled?
